## What is the natural question?

Earlier bilingual activation studies already know homographs activate senses across languages under many tasks, but task and global stimulus list jointly alter the amount of interference. **Is this change due to the lexical access system, or the response/decision system?** The model family BIA+/Multilink+ explicitly separates these explanatory levels.

## Idea source and innovation scale (INFERENCE)

Instead of one more pure versus mixed bilingual study, the authors cross **three list compositions** (L2-only English; L1 Dutch + L2 English; L2 English + L3 French) with **two tasks** (lexical decision vs go/no-go) in six experiments. The novel L2–L3 context helps isolate whether the mere presence of a second language, or a strong competing L1 reading, creates interference. Task changes response mapping and conflict requirements. This is a **theoretically motivated factorial comparison** on a heavily studied phenomenon, not a blank research space.

## Verified results

- 6 experiments, trilinguals dominant Dutch L1, English L2, French L3; target English. Linear mixed-effects analyses across RT and errors.
- Homograph inhibition is present in pure and mixed-list conditions; mixed L1–L2 lists show far larger inhibition.
- Go/no-go produces larger inhibition on RT, but the reported third-order interaction of word type × list × task was not significant; task does not wholly reconfigure the ordering of list conditions.
- Error response rates have another pattern (go/no-go can reduce errors), so “more inhibition” cannot be reduced to one number.
- Table2 example: lexical decision in L1–L2 mixed list RT homograph 702ms vs control 594ms; go/no-go 781ms vs control 607ms. These reflect human reaction latency and learned response schemes, not model perplexity.

## Why the theory got deeper (INFERENCE)

A basic "both language senses activate" theory predicts more competition but doesn't explain why task-specific go/no-go response mappings and distractor-language list manipulations change the amount and direction of measurable inhibition. The authors link observed effects to a **task/decision layer** alongside lexical retrieval, refining earlier BIA+/Multilink+ rather than declaring broad language nonselectivity new.

## Rival accounts and limits

General task difficulty, motor response latency, language dominance, response inhibition, list-induced bilingual mode and lexical sense frequency each predict some effects. Pure L2 still exhibits inhibition; this weakens a model claiming only mixed-language items cause coactivation. Task differences in mean RT do not prove different representations have been activated; need independent process evidence. The design is between groups across different tasks, and variation in trilingual proficiency can affect inference.

## Closest to our LLM experiment

A word-language answer Q1 and word-sense answer Q2 are not neutral measurements of one shared hidden "language tag." Their task decisions can interact; however, human response delays do not imply transformers implement Multilink or BIA+. Our next cross-task semantic outcome requires **identical lexical instance, equal evidence, only task and output arrangement changed**.

## What would go beyond this paper?

Predict conditions under which independent word-language cue and semantic-context evidence jointly drive *conditional model sense preferences*, with no lexical-copy artifact. To interpret a new LLM interaction, control instruction priming / generated Q1 commitment and monolingual semantic ambiguity; do not report human RT similarity as internal mechanistic evidence.
